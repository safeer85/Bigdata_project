\chapter{Introduction}
\label{ch:introduction}

A ride-hailing operator runs two businesses at once. One is an
operations business, conducted minute by minute: where are the cars, how
many are earning, which zones are hot, which driver has been sitting
still for an hour. The other is a financial business, conducted a day at
a time: what did each vehicle earn, what did it cost to run, and is it
still worth keeping on the road. This project builds a single data
platform that answers both, and argues that the interesting engineering
decision is not how to answer either one but how to answer both without
letting the fast answer contaminate the exact one.

\section{Background}
\label{sec:background}

The assessment brief~\cite{ec8203brief} specifies a use case with two
ingestion paths of deliberately different character. A streaming source
emits GPS and telemetry events per vehicle every few seconds:
\code{trip\_id}, \code{driver\_id}, \code{vehicle\_id}, latitude,
longitude, speed, status, fare and timestamp. A daily batch source drops
one file per simulated day carrying vehicle expense records from garages
and fuel partners: \code{vehicle\_id}, \code{fuel\_cost},
\code{maintenance\_cost}, \code{distance\_covered} and
\code{service\_flag}.

These two paths differ in more than cadence. The telemetry stream is
high-volume, continuous, owned by us, and tolerant of small losses. The
expense file is low-volume, periodic, owned by a third party, and
\emph{authoritative about money}. It can also be wrong, and be corrected:
a fuel-card partner that mis-keys a reconciliation will resubmit a
corrected file days later, and the figures we published in the meantime
must be restated. That asymmetry, more than any latency requirement,
drives the architecture in this report.

\section{Problem Statement}
\label{sec:problem}

The brief poses one business question:

\begin{quote}
What is fleet utilisation and earnings by area and time of day right
now, and which vehicles are becoming unprofitable once yesterday's fuel
and maintenance costs are factored in?
\end{quote}

Read carefully, that is two questions whose non-functional requirements
are mutually incompatible, as Table~\ref{tab:two-questions} sets out.

\begin{table}[htbp]
\centering
\caption{The brief contains two sub-questions with incompatible
requirements. No single processing model serves both well.}
\label{tab:two-questions}
\begin{tabularx}{\textwidth}{@{}lXX@{}}
\toprule
& \textbf{Live utilisation} & \textbf{Daily profitability}\\
\midrule
Latency required    & seconds & next morning is acceptable\\
Accuracy required   & approximate is fine & exact --- it is money\\
Completeness        & best effort & must cover the whole day\\
Recomputable?       & no need & \textbf{yes} --- partners resubmit
                                corrected files\\
Consumer            & dispatcher watching a map & finance, making
                                retire/keep decisions\\
\bottomrule
\end{tabularx}
\end{table}

A dispatcher deciding where to send a car needs an answer in seconds and
does not care if one trip is missing. A finance analyst deciding whether
to retire a vehicle needs a figure that is complete, exact and
\emph{reproducible}, because the fuel-card partner can and does resubmit
a corrected expense file days later. Serving both from one processing
model means either making the dispatcher wait for exactness they do not
need, or letting finance quote a number whose error is unbounded and
unknown. The problem this project solves is how to provide both
guarantees from one dataset while making it impossible for a consumer to
confuse them.

\section{Objectives and Scope}
\label{sec:objectives}

\subsection*{Objectives}

\begin{enumerate}
  \item Choose between a Lambda and a Kappa architecture on the
        evidence of this use case's latency, replay, cost and
        consistency requirements, and defend the rejected alternative
        specifically rather than dismissively.
  \item Implement both ingestion paths as simulated Python sources: a
        continuous Kafka producer and a once-per-simulated-day file
        drop, with deliberate faults so that error handling can be
        demonstrated rather than asserted.
  \item Implement processing consistent with the declared architecture,
        performing meaningful transformation --- cleaning, zone
        enrichment, windowed aggregation, stateful alerting, and a join
        between the two sources --- not pass-through.
  \item Land results in a queryable store and serve a consolidated
        daily report and a live dashboard that answer the business
        question.
  \item Instrument every stage with structured logging, metrics and
        alerting, so the pipeline's health and its \emph{known
        imprecision} are both observable.
  \item Quantify the cost of the architecture's approximation rather
        than assuming it, and report the measurement honestly whichever
        way it falls.
\end{enumerate}

\subsection*{Scope and simplifications}

The system runs on a single 16\,GB laptop from one command
(\code{make up}) and is demonstrable inside a lab session. Three
simplifications follow from that, and each is stated where it matters
rather than buried: simulated time is compressed 60-fold, so one real
second is one simulated minute and a simulated day takes 24 real
minutes; Kafka runs as a single broker with replication factor~1; and
batch Spark runs in local mode, so the project demonstrates distributed
\emph{streaming} but not distributed \emph{batch}. Authentication,
multi-tenancy and cost-based query optimisation are out of scope.
Chapter~\ref{ch:discussion} sets out what each simplification would
become at production scale.

\section{Contributions}
\label{sec:contributions}

\begin{enumerate}
  \item \textbf{An architecture decision argued from the correction
        channel, not from latency.} We show that the usual Lambda-versus-Kappa
        comparison --- which turns on replay cost --- resolves differently
        here because the event that forces recomputation never enters the
        event log at all (Section~\ref{sec:kappa-rejected}).
  \item \textbf{A structural mitigation for Lambda's two-codebase
        problem}, with the two deliberate duplications pinned together by
        tests that feed identical inputs through both implementations
        (Section~\ref{sec:lambda-cost}).
  \item \textbf{A measured, rather than asserted, account of speed-layer
        error.} We report a revenue drift of zero on three days of four
        and explain arithmetically why zero is the expected outcome,
        instead of inflating the injected fault rate to produce a more
        impressive number (Section~\ref{sec:consistency}).
  \item \textbf{Six defects found by operating the system}, each
        generalising beyond this project, including a healthcheck that
        stayed green over an archiver that had stopped writing
        (Section~\ref{sec:defects}).
\end{enumerate}

\section{Report Organisation}
\label{sec:organisation}

Chapter~\ref{ch:literature} reviews the Lambda and Kappa positions and
the stream-processing semantics the implementation relies on.
Chapter~\ref{ch:architecture} states the architecture decision, the
rejected alternatives, and the technology chosen for each layer with its
justification. Chapter~\ref{ch:implementation} describes the
implementation stage by stage: ingestion, speed layer, batch layer and
orchestration, storage and serving, and observability.
Chapter~\ref{ch:results} presents the measured results, with the
speed-versus-batch consistency evidence as its core.
Chapter~\ref{ch:discussion} discusses what operating the system taught
us, the limitations and threats to validity, and what would change at
production scale. Chapter~\ref{ch:conclusion} concludes. The appendices
carry the repository layout, the database schema, the alert inventory,
the API surface and the statement of individual contributions.
