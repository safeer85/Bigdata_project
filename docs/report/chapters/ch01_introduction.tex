\chapter{Introduction}
\label{ch:introduction}

A ride-hailing operator runs two businesses at once. One is an operations
business, conducted minute by minute: where are the cars, how many are
earning, which driver has been sitting still for an hour. The other is a
financial business, conducted a day at a time: what did each vehicle
earn, what did it cost to run, and is it still worth keeping on the road.
This project builds one platform that answers both, and argues that the
interesting decision is not how to answer either but how to answer both
without letting the fast answer contaminate the exact one.

\section{Background and Problem Statement}
\label{sec:problem}

The brief~\cite{ec8203brief} specifies two ingestion paths that behave
very differently. A streaming source emits GPS and telemetry events per
vehicle every few seconds
(\code{trip\_id}, \code{driver\_id}, \code{vehicle\_id}, latitude,
longitude, speed, status, fare, timestamp). A daily batch source drops
one file per simulated day carrying expense records from garages and fuel
partners (\code{vehicle\_id}, \code{fuel\_cost},
\code{maintenance\_cost}, \code{distance\_covered},
\code{service\_flag}).

They differ in more than cadence. The telemetry stream is high-volume,
continuous, owned by us, and tolerant of small losses. The expense file
is low-volume, periodic, owned by a third party, and \emph{authoritative
about money}. It can also be wrong and be corrected: a fuel-card partner
that mis-keys a reconciliation resubmits days later, and the figures
published in the meantime must be restated. That asymmetry, more than any
latency requirement, drives everything that follows.

The brief poses what looks like one business question: \emph{what is
fleet utilisation and earnings by area and time of day right now, and
which vehicles are becoming unprofitable once yesterday's fuel and
maintenance costs are factored in?} It is really two, and their
requirements conflict, as Table~\ref{tab:two-questions} sets out.

\begin{table}[htbp]
\centering
\caption{The brief contains two sub-questions with incompatible
requirements. No single processing model serves both well.}
\label{tab:two-questions}
\begin{tabularx}{\textwidth}{@{}lXX@{}}
\toprule
& \textbf{Live utilisation} & \textbf{Daily profitability}\\
\midrule
Latency required    & seconds & next morning is acceptable\\
Accuracy required   & approximate is fine & exact; it is money\\
Completeness        & best effort & must cover the whole day\\
Recomputable?       & no need & \textbf{yes}, because partners resubmit
                                corrected files\\
Consumer            & dispatcher watching a map & finance, making
                                retire/keep decisions\\
\bottomrule
\end{tabularx}
\end{table}

A dispatcher deciding where to send a car needs an answer in seconds and
does not care if one trip is missing. A finance analyst deciding whether
to retire a vehicle needs a figure that is complete, exact and
\emph{reproducible}. Serving both from one processing model means either
making the dispatcher wait for exactness they do not need, or letting
finance quote a number whose error is unbounded and unknown. The problem
is to provide both guarantees from one dataset while making it impossible
for a consumer to confuse them.

\section{Objectives and Scope}
\label{sec:objectives}

We set out to choose between Lambda and Kappa on the evidence of this use
case, and to say exactly why the loser lost. Both ingestion paths are
simulated Python sources that inject faults into their own output, so the
error handling can be shown working instead of claimed. The processing
does real work on the data: cleaning, zone enrichment, windowed
aggregation, stateful alerting, and a join across the two sources.
Results land in a queryable store behind a live dashboard and a
consolidated daily report. Every stage is instrumented, and what is
instrumented includes the pipeline's \emph{known imprecision}, not just
its health. That last point turned out to be the most interesting part of
the project, so we measured the imprecision rather than assuming a value
for it.

Three simplifications follow from running the whole system on a single
16\,GB laptop from one command. Simulated time is compressed 60-fold, so
one real second is one simulated minute and a simulated day takes 24 real
minutes (Section~\ref{sec:simclock}). Kafka runs as a single broker with
replication factor~1. Batch Spark runs in local mode, so the project
demonstrates distributed \emph{streaming} but not distributed
\emph{batch}. Authentication and multi-tenancy are out of scope.
Section~\ref{sec:production} states what each would become in
production.
